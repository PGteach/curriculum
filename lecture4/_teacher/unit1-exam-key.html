Section B — marking scheme
21What is meant by algorithmic bias in AI systems? Give one example of it.
1-4 — Ministry assessments, lesson 1-4, class tasks, essay 1; Book p.27
[2 marks]

Model answer. Algorithmic bias is bias in AI judgments caused by bias in the training data. Example: a hiring AI that unfairly evaluates a particular gender, or a face-recognition AI that is more likely to misidentify particular ethnic groups.

Award a mark for each:

  • Correct definition, linked to training data (1)
  • One of the book's two examples (1)
22What is meant by Moore's Law? Is it a fixed law of physics? Explain your answer.
1-1 — Ministry assessments, lesson 1-1, class tasks, essay 2; Book p.8 Worked Example (2) A-B
[3 marks]

Model answer. Moore's Law is the observation that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles approximately every two years. It is not a fixed law of physics: it is an empirical observation of what has happened, and in recent years it has been said to be approaching a physical limit.

Award a mark for each:

  • Transistors on an integrated circuit double (1)
  • Approximately every two years (1)
  • Not a law of physics — an empirical observation, said to be nearing a physical limit (1)
23Briefly compare AR (augmented reality) and VR (virtual reality) in terms of definition and how they work.
1-1 — Ministry assessments, lesson 1-1, class tasks (period 2), essay 2; Book p.8 Worked Example (3)
[3 marks]

Model answer. AR overlays digital information on real-world images, so the user still sees the real world with a digital layer added on top. VR immerses the user in a virtual space generated by a computer, replacing the real world with that space.

Award a mark for each:

  • AR overlays digital information on real-world images (1)
  • VR immerses the user in a computer-generated virtual space (1)
  • The contrast: AR adds to the real world; VR replaces it (1)

24What is meant by a hallucination in generative AI? Explain why it is dangerous to use such output as the answer to a school report as-is, and state what learners should do instead.
1-2 — Book p.16 Think It Through; Ministry assessments, lesson 1-2, class tasks (period 2), essay 2, and weekly model A, essay 4
[3 marks]

Model answer. A hallucination is text produced by generative AI that sounds plausible but is factually incorrect. It is dangerous in a school report because the error looks convincing, so it can be copied in as fact without being noticed. Learners should check the output against reliable sources before using it.

Award a mark for each:

  • Definition: plausible-sounding but factually incorrect output (1)
  • Danger: it looks convincing, so errors go unnoticed into the report (1)
  • What to do: verify the output and its sources before using it (1)
25A hospital is considering using image-diagnosis AI to detect diseases from X-ray images. Explain why the final diagnosis should be confirmed by a human doctor rather than left to AI alone.
1-3 — Book p.21 Think It Through; Ministry assessments, lesson 1-3, weekly model B, essay 4
[3 marks]

Model answer. It can be unclear how the AI reached its judgment — the black-box problem — so its result is hard to verify. A wrong diagnosis can harm a patient, and final decision-making needs someone who is responsible for the result, which the AI cannot be. So a doctor should confirm it.

Award a mark for each:

  • How the AI decided can be unclear (black box), so the result is hard to verify (1)
  • A wrong result can harm the patient (1)
  • Final decisions need a responsible human; the AI cannot be answerable (1)

26Analyze why AI is well suited to some tasks but requires caution in others. In your answer, refer to what AI is good at (finding patterns, recognition, prediction) and to what needs caution (ethical judgments, privacy, final decision-making).
1-3 / 1-4 — Book p.22 Exam-style question [6]; Ministry assessments, lesson 1-3, weekly model A, essay 4
[6 marks]

Model answer. AI is well suited to tasks built on patterns in data: it can find and classify patterns in complex data such as images and text, recognise and generate images, audio and text, and make probabilistic predictions based on data — for example a recommendation system or image-diagnosis AI. It requires caution where those strengths stop being enough. Ethical judgments can lead to discrimination or prejudice, especially when the training data is biased, so they should not be left entirely to AI. Judgments involving personal information raise privacy issues. And final decision-making needs someone who is responsible for the result, while the AI's reasoning may be a black box. So AI is a powerful tool for pattern-based tasks, but human judgment is still needed for ethics, privacy and responsibility.

Award a mark for each:

  • What AI is good at — patterns, recognition/generation, prediction — at least two (1)
  • Why: these tasks are based on patterns in data, with an example (1)
  • Ethical judgments: risk of discrimination or prejudice, linked to biased training data (1)
  • Privacy: judgments involving personal information (1)
  • Final decision-making: responsibility, and/or the black-box problem (1)
  • A conclusion that weighs the two sides — a tool for patterns, with human judgment kept for ethics, privacy and responsibility (1)

Marking. Six points, one mark each. A plain list of strengths and cautions with no reasoning earns no more than 3.

Total: 40 marks. Section A 20, Section B 20. Accept any wording that carries the point; the book’s words are not required, the idea is.
